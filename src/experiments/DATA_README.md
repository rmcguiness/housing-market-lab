# Sweep data — what's in it and what to trust

Generated by `npm run sweep` (writes CSVs to `data/`). Every figure is averaged
over 5 seeds to damp stochastic noise. Model: agent-based assignment market, ~1,500
households / 1,320 units, NYC-calibrated. **This is a mechanism model, not a
calibrated forecaster — read the reliability tiers before quoting numbers.**

## Files

### `snapshot_policies.csv` — one-shot comparative statics
Free market vs. rent ceiling (−30%) vs. light/heavy assistance, crossed with
property-tax levels {0, 1.2%, 4%}. Columns: `housedPct`, `medianRent`,
`rentBurdenedPct`, `landlordProfit`, `consumerSurplus`, `producerSurplus`,
`totalSurplus`, `deadweightLoss`, `assistanceCost`, `giniIncome`, and
`q1..q5_pricedOutPct` (priced-out rate by income quintile, Q1 = poorest).

### `overtime_endpoints.csv` — 25-year dynamics, fully crossed
Free market vs. rent freeze × property tax {0, 2%, 4%} × income tax {0, 5%, 10%}
× voucher {$0, $1500} × trickle {0, 0.5} = 72 rows. Start/end totals for supply,
housing, population, rent, income, revenue, abandonment, and end-state priced-out
for Q1/Q3/Q5.

### `trickle_income_groups.csv` — distributional effect of trickle-down
Trickle strength {0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1.0} × {free market, rent freeze}. Per-quintile
income growth and end housing, plus **start/end Gini and top-income share**.

## Reliability tiers — IMPORTANT

**Tier 1 — robust (textbook + empirical support; safe to feature):**
- Rent ceiling below market → fewer housed, lower rents for the lucky, **positive
  deadweight loss**, and units withdrawn (`snapshot_policies.csv`).
- Higher property tax → fewer housed, higher rents, lower landlord profit
  (monotone across the three tax levels).
- Assistance (vouchers + public housing) → sharply lower **Q1** priced-out, at a
  government cost, with some rent capitalization (`medianRent` under light
  assistance vs. free market).
- Trickle-down strength → **rising Gini and top-income share** (monotone). This is
  the cleanest distributional result: the growth is top-weighted by construction,
  so more strength = more inequality.

**Tier 2 — directional only (illustrative, sensitive to assumptions):**
- Over-time *aggregate* supply / housed / revenue trajectories. The sign is
  meaningful; the magnitude depends on contested behavioural sliders (landlord
  flight, emigration, construction elasticity).

**Tier 3 — do NOT anchor claims on these (confounded):**
- Over-time **per-quintile** housing (`overtime_endpoints.csv` `end_qN_pricedOut`)
  and per-quintile income *growth* (`trickle_income_groups.csv` `qN_incomeGrowthPct`).
  Quintiles are recomputed on the *current* population each year, and emigration
  removes top earners, so these mix a real effect with a composition shift. Example
  artifact: free market + high tax can show Q1 *better* housed than with no tax —
  that's the population thinning, not taxes helping the poor. Use **Gini /
  top-income share** for over-time inequality instead; they're composition-robust.

## Caveats that apply to everything
- Rent freeze is modelled as universal (real freezes hit only rent-stabilized
  units, ~half the stock).
- Vouchers are locally funded here (Section 8 is federal), which strengthens the
  fiscal-feedback loop relative to reality.
- Distress shows up as exclusion (priced out), not over-budget rent burden.
- See the in-app Methodology → Limitations for the full list.
