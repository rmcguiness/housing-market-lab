# X / Twitter post — "Mamdani's housing plan is a bet on government execution"

Focused on the three claims that hold up under scrutiny, each sourced:
1. Freezing rent + taxing landlords shrinks rental supply.
2. Government can't maintain the housing it owns (NYCHA).
3. Vouchers raise rents for the people who don't get them.

Thesis: every leg of the plan depends on the same missing ingredient — **new
supply** — so the whole thing rides on a build-out that government has a poor track
record of delivering.

Two versions below + a sourced reference list. Everything here is defensible.

## VERSION A — Thread (13 posts)

**1/**
Mamdani's housing plan is a bet on the one thing government has the worst track
record at: building housing — and maintaining it.

I built an open simulator and pulled the actual research. Here's what holds up. 🧵

**2/**
The plan, in one line: freeze rents on stabilized apartments, tax landlords and the
wealthy more, and use the money to build and run affordable housing.

The freeze and the tax are the easy part. "Build and maintain" is the part that has
to work — and it's historically the hard part.

**3/**
① Freezing rent shrinks the supply it's trying to protect.

Stanford's study of San Francisco rent control (Diamond, McQuade & Qian, *American
Economic Review* 2019) found it *cut* rental supply ~15% — landlords converted to
condos or sold to owner-occupiers. Citywide rents actually *rose*.

**4/**
Now add higher property taxes. They bite the thinnest-margin landlords first — the
ones operating the cheap units. The policy aimed at protecting affordable rentals
is the one most likely to retire them.

That's not a hot take; it's what the model and the data both show.

**5/**
② Government is a famously bad landlord — and you don't have to theorize it.

NYCHA, NYC's existing public housing, has a capital-repair backlog that grew from
~$32 billion (2017) to ~$78 billion (2023) by its *own* assessments. Mold, broken
heat, lead paint. 400,000+ residents.

**6/**
That's the maintenance trap: below-cost rents don't cover upkeep → the gap falls on
a city budget that's always being fought over → the buildings rot.

Expanding public ownership expands that liability. You'd be adding buildings to a
system already ~$78B underwater.

**7/**
③ Vouchers can raise rents for the people who *don't* get them.

Susin (*Journal of Public Economics*, 2002) found housing vouchers pushed up rents
for low-income *non-recipients* — in aggregate, the rent increase *exceeded* the
subsidy. A transfer to landlords, paid by the unsubsidized poor.

**8/**
Why? Textbook incidence. Subsidize demand in a market where you can't quickly add
supply, and the subsidy gets capitalized into price.

Vouchers without building = higher rents for everyone competing for the same scarce
apartments — including the working people just above the cutoff.

**9/**
Notice the pattern. All three legs — freeze, public housing, vouchers — fail in the
*same* way: they move money and prices around a fixed amount of housing.

Every one of them depends on the one ingredient the plan can't guarantee: a lot of
new supply, fast.

**10/**
So I ran the plan two ways in the model:

• Build-out *delivers* → modest growth, more people housed.
• Build-out *underdelivers* (the historical norm) → the rental stock falls sharply.

Same plan. The entire outcome hinges on execution.

**11/**
And the execution bar is 200,000 units. NYC has not built at anything close to that
pace in modern history. That's the wager — not "socialism vs. capitalism" in the
abstract, but a concrete bet that government will build and maintain at a rate it
has never hit.

**12/**
To be fair — and the model shows this too — the free market, left alone, prices the
bottom of the market out. That's the real problem that created this fight.

So the honest question isn't price controls vs. nothing. It's: do you fix supply by
*building*, or by *capping price* — which the evidence says shrinks supply and lets
it decay?

## VERSION B — Single long post

Mamdani's housing plan is a bet on the one thing government does worst: building and
maintaining housing. I built an open simulator and pulled the research. Three things
hold up.

**One: freezing rent shrinks the supply it's meant to protect.** Stanford's study of
SF rent control (Diamond, McQuade & Qian, AER 2019) found it cut rental supply ~15%
as landlords exited to condos and owner-occupancy — and pushed citywide rents up.
Layer on higher property taxes and the thinnest-margin landlords, running the cheap
units, go first.

**Two: government can't maintain what it owns.** You don't have to theorize it —
NYCHA's repair backlog grew from ~$32B (2017) to ~$78B (2023) by its own count.
Below-cost rents don't fund upkeep; the gap falls on a contested budget; the
buildings decay. Expanding public ownership expands that liability.

**Three: vouchers raise rents for the people who don't get them.** Susin (J. Public
Econ, 2002) found vouchers raised rents for low-income non-recipients by more, in
aggregate, than the subsidy itself — a transfer to landlords paid by the unsubsidized
poor. It's basic incidence: subsidize demand without adding supply and the money
capitalizes into price.

All three fail the same way: they rearrange a fixed amount of housing. The plan only
works if it builds — a lot, fast. The bar is 200,000 units; NYC has never built at
that pace. Run it in the simulator with the build-out succeeding or failing and you
can watch the whole result flip on that one variable.

In fairness, the free market alone prices the poor out — that's the problem that
started this. The real question isn't controls vs. nothing. It's whether you fix
supply by building, or by capping price, which the evidence says shrinks and decays
it. [link]

## SOURCES (post as replies / link out)

1. **Rent control shrinks supply:** Diamond, R., McQuade, T., & Qian, F. (2019).
   "The Effects of Rent Control Expansion on Tenants, Landlords, and Inequality:
   Evidence from San Francisco." *American Economic Review*, 109(9), 3365–3394.
   (Found a ~15% reduction in rental housing supply; citywide rent increases.)

2. **Public-housing maintenance crisis:** New York City Housing Authority, *Physical
   Needs Assessment* (2017, ~$32B) and 2023 update (~$78B in capital needs). Widely
   reported by the NYC Comptroller and city press.

3. **Vouchers capitalize into rents for non-recipients:** Susin, S. (2002). "Rent
   vouchers and the price of low-income housing." *Journal of Public Economics*,
   83(1), 109–152.

4. **Demand subsidies + inelastic supply → price capitalization** is the standard
   incidence result in any public-economics text (e.g., Rosen & Gayer, *Public
   Finance*).

(Optional, if challenged on "the wealthy will flee": be careful — Young et al.
(2016), *American Sociological Review*, find millionaire migration in response to
tax is *small*. Don't lean on capital flight; it's your weakest claim.)

### Notes for you (not for posting)

  capped at ~the 40th-percentile local rent ("Fair Market Rent") — not "nicer
  houses." Stated this way, point ③ is *stronger* and unimpeachable: even a modest,
  well-targeted voucher still bids up rents on the unsubsidized poor.

  makes you easier to dismiss, not harder.
